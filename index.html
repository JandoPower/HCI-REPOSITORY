Lecture 1

Introduction to HCI

What is HCI? Three eras

In plain words

HCI stands for Human-Computer Interaction: the study of how people and computers work together, and how to make computers easy and comfortable to use. The lecture tells its story in three eras. Traditional HCI asked one simple question: is this system convenient and easy for people to use? After the 2000s, the field grew to cover many people using digital systems in many ways, and how both single people and groups can get the best experience. After the 2020s, AI arrived: systems that learn from data, judge the situation and act on their own. This is called HAII (Human-AI Interaction).

Example: Traditional: choosing the background color, where the buttons go, and what sound plays when you click. 2000s: social media, or checking live traffic on your phone. 2020s: an AI in a car, a bank or a hospital, where the kind of input, output and interaction changes with the area it works in.

Remember: Three eras in one line: easy to use (buttons, colors, sounds) → many people + digital systems (social media, live traffic) → AI that decides for itself. But the old, proven design principles still matter in every era.

EraFocusExample
TraditionalStudying how to develop systems that are convenient and easy to use; designing and evaluating for ease of useBackground color, button placement, sound effects
After 2000sScope expands to many users interacting with digital systems; how individuals and groups get an optimal UXSocial networking, checking traffic
After 2020sAI: systems learn from data, assess situations, act autonomously. HAII (Human-AI Interaction): the domain (car, finance, health) changes input, output, processing, and the form of interactionDomain-specific AI

Even so, interaction design principles validated in the past are still important.

UI, UX, usability

In plain words

Picture a coffee shop. The counter, the menu board and the card reader are the parts you see and touch that connect you to the shop. On a computer, that bridge is the UI (user interface): screens, buttons, keyboards. Everything you think and feel about the visit, before (looking forward to it), during (was the line slow?) and after (was it worth it?), is the UX (user experience). Usability asks the simple question: did you actually get what you came for, even if the menu was complicated?

Example: The lecture's chain: a Cancel button sits in a strange spot (interface) → you tap it by mistake (interaction) → you feel annoyed and have to do it all again (experience).

Remember: UI = what you touch. UX = what you feel (before, during, after). Usability = did you reach your goal? Chain: Interface → Interaction → Experience.

  • User Interface (UI): software/hardware that bridges human action and computer action. Maps the sensory, cognitive and social human world onto computational functions (Andy Ko).
  • User Experience (UX) (ISO 9241-210:2010): a user's perceptions and responses that result from the use and/or anticipated use of a system, product or service. Covers emotions, beliefs, preferences, perceptions, comfort, behaviors and accomplishments before, during and after use.
  • Relationship: User ↔ Experience ↔ Interaction ↔ Interface ↔ System/Data.
    Example: a button sits in an unexpected place (interface) → I click Cancel by mistake (interaction) → I'm annoyed and have to redo it (experience).
  • Usability: everything that supports achieving the user's goal when using a system, beyond basic functional convenience. Focus: can users accurately accomplish their goal despite the system's complexity? Developing usable products is the central concern of interaction design.

Switching to digital

In plain words

Lots of things we used to do with paper, coins or people are now done with apps and screens. The good side: it is easier, quicker and more convenient, and it saves companies money. The bad side: people who cannot use the technology get left out, and it stops working when the battery or the network dies.

Example: A car park that only accepts payment through an app: great if your phone works, but useless if your battery just died or you don't have a smartphone.

Remember: Digital = faster and cheaper, but only for people who can use it, and only while the battery lasts.

Pros: easier, quicker, more convenient; cost-efficient for companies. Cons: people who can't use the technology get left out, and it depends on things like battery and network.

What to design

In plain words

Before you design anything interactive, ask four questions: who will use it, how will they use it, when and where, and what are they doing at that moment. The big question of interaction design is: how do we make someone's use of a product effective, useful, usable and pleasurable? To answer it you must understand people: what they are good and bad at, what would help them, what gives a good experience, their privacy worries. You should also listen to them and involve them in the design.

Example: A recipe app: a home cook (who), on a tablet (how), in the kitchen at dinner time (when and where), stirring a pot with messy hands (activity). Knowing that, big buttons or voice control start to make sense.

Remember: Four questions: Who? How? When and where? Doing what? Key question's four words: effective, useful, usable, pleasurable.

Consider: (1) who will use it, (2) how it will be used, (3) when and where, (4) what activities people are doing while interacting.

Key question of interaction design“How do you optimize a person's interactions with a system, environment, or product so that they support their activities in effective, useful, usable, and pleasurable ways?”

Requires understanding people: what they're good and bad at; what helps the way they currently do things; what gives quality experiences; privacy concerns; listening and involving them; people-centered techniques.

Interaction design (IxD)

In plain words

Interaction design (IxD) means designing interactive products (things that respond to you, like apps, kiosks and smart devices) so they fit how people communicate and interact in daily life and at work. The goal is experiences that make people's work, communication and interaction better. It differs from related fields in its methods, philosophies and lenses (ways of looking at a problem) and in the scope of problems it tackles. It is done by multidisciplinary teams: people from many fields, from social scientists to movie-makers, working together.

Example: Building an information kiosk for a science museum might need a programmer, a graphic designer, someone who understands how visitors think, and a museum expert. You get more ideas, but also more meetings and more cost.

Remember: Mixed team: + more creative ideas and new methods; − more cost, harder to communicate and make progress.

  • Definition: “Designing interactive products to support the way people communicate and interact in their everyday and working lives.” Creating experiences that enhance and augment how people work, communicate and interact.
  • Differs from related fields in methods, philosophies and lenses, and in the scope and problems addressed.
  • Done by multidisciplinary teams (social scientists to movie-makers). (+) more creative ideas, new methods. (−) cost, hard to communicate and make progress. Interaction design consultancies can help.

People-centered design

In plain words

People-centered design is a way of thinking where the people who will use the product come first: their needs, their wishes and their feelings. It is not only about whether something works, but whether it feels good, looks good and is pleasant to use, down to small details. Don Norman put it this way: working, understandable and usable is not enough; products should also bring joy, fun and beauty into people's lives.

Example: Two music players can both play songs. If one also feels smooth when you scroll, looks beautiful and is fun to use, people love it more. The lecture's “Apple phenomenon” discussion (why was the iPod such a hit?) asks you to think about this extra something.

Remember: Three names, three ideas: Hornbæk & Hertzum = how people see it + how they feel (is the smartwatch cool?); Marc et al. = hedonic (pleasure) side and well-being; McCarthy & Wright = expectations and how people make sense of them.

  • A design philosophy that prioritizes the needs and desires of the people who will use a product. Includes how people feel about it: pleasure, satisfaction, overall impression, small details.
  • Don Norman: functional, understandable and usable isn't enough; we must also build joy, excitement, pleasure, fun and beauty into people's lives.
  • Aspects: usability, functionality, aesthetics, content, look and feel, emotional appeal (+ fun, health, social capital, cultural identity, education).
  • Experiential aspects: Hornbæk & Hertzum: how people perceive a product (is the smartwatch cool?) + their emotional reaction. Marc et al.: hedonic aspects related to well-being. McCarthy & Wright: people's expectations and how they make sense of their experiences.

Understanding people

In plain words

Designers need to know the people they design for: how they live, work and learn. People differ (children, teenagers and adults need different things), so one design may not fit all. Learning about real people can also show that our guesses were wrong. Things to consider include age, culture and accessibility.

Example: Culture: one country writes dates as MM/DD/YY (month first), another as YY/MM/DD (year first). The same numbers can mean different days to different people, so a date field must fit the user's culture.

Remember: Don't guess, check. The lecture's example: people often assume older adults want everything large. Is that really true? Ask real users.

Helps design products that augment people and match their needs. Designers must understand individual differences (children, teens, adults), and learning about people can reveal incorrect assumptions (do older adults really want everything large?). Consider age (older adults may resist new tech), culture (MM/DD/YY vs YY/MM/DD), accessibility.

Accessibility & inclusiveness

In plain words

Accessibility asks: how many people can use this, with a special focus on people with disabilities? Inclusiveness is wider: being fair, open and equal to everyone, so the product fits the widest possible number of people. There are two ways to get there: inclusive design (build it for everyone from the start) and assistive technology (extra tools such as a screen reader). Impairments can be sensory (seeing, hearing), physical (the body) or cognitive (memory, thinking), and they can also be permanent, temporary or situational.

Example: Video captions help a deaf person (permanent), someone with a bad ear infection (temporary), and someone on a noisy bus (situational). The lecture's own example: text messaging (SMS) was first designed for hearing-impaired people, and now everyone uses it.

Remember: Accessibility = focus on disability. Inclusiveness = everyone. Key number: fewer than 20% are born with a disability, but 80% will have one by age 85.

Accessibility

The extent to which a product is accessible by as many people as possible. Focus is on people with disabilities. Google/Apple give developers tools (screen readers, VoiceOver, hearing-aid compatibility).

Inclusiveness

Being fair, open and equal to everyone. Inclusive design is the overarching approach to accommodate the widest possible number of people.
  • Why it matters: tech that requires one type of interaction can be impossible for someone with a disability, which is poor interaction design. Accessibility opens experiences to all (SMS was first designed for hearing-impaired people).
  • Two ways to achieve it: inclusive design and assistive technology.
  • Impairment types: sensory (vision/hearing), physical (loss of body parts/function), cognitive (memory, e.g. Alzheimer's). Each type is a complex mix (peripheral vision, color blindness, no light perception…).
  • Also permanent / temporary / situational.
  • Fewer than 20% are born with a disability, but 80% will have one by age 85.
  • Assistive tech is life-essential and an extension of the self (Holloway & Dawes). DIX (Disability Interactions): cross-disciplinary methods from HCI and disability. IoT, wearables, VR can improve existing assistive tech.

Usability goals

In plain words

Usability goals are a checklist for judging whether a product is good to use. Effectiveness: does it do its job? Efficiency: how many steps does it take? Safety: does it protect you and let you recover from mistakes? Utility: does it have the functions you need? Learnability: can a newcomer figure it out by exploring? Memorability: can you remember how to use it after a break? Satisfaction: do people find it acceptable and like it? Separately, UX goals are about how it feels, and whether a feeling is good or bad depends on the situation.

Example: A pizza-delivery app. Effective: you can order pizza. Efficient: 3 taps, not 15. Safe: you can cancel a wrong order. Utility: it has address, payment and tracking. Learnable: first-timers manage. Memorable: you still know how after a month. Satisfying: users rate it highly.

Remember: Learnability = the first time. Memorability = coming back later. Effectiveness = can it do the job? Efficiency = how many steps?

UX goals: desirable vs undesirable aspects depend on the situation (music in the shower vs in the car). Even a single action at the interface can affect UX quality.

Design principles

In plain words

Design principles are six rules that help a product explain itself, so nobody needs a manual. Visibility: show people what they can do next. Feedback: tell people what just happened. Constraints: block choices that make no sense right now. Mapping: controls clearly match the real things they control. Consistency: similar tasks are done in a similar way. Affordance: the look or shape gives a clue about how to use it.

Example: A phone: icons you can see (visibility), a whoosh sound when a message sends (feedback), a grey Send button while the message is empty (constraints), volume-up button above volume-down (mapping), the back arrow in the same place in every app (consistency), a slider that looks draggable (affordance).

Remember: See it (Visibility), hear back (Feedback), can't go wrong (Constraints), matches the layout (Mapping), same everywhere (Consistency), shape gives a clue (Affordance).

Lecture 2

The Process of Interaction Design

Interaction design involves: discovering requirements, designing to fulfill them, and producing prototypes for evaluation. Identifying, understanding and engaging stakeholders is fundamental. People-centered concerns come before technical ones.

The Double Diamond

In plain words

Think about planning a class party. First you ask lots of classmates what they would enjoy (you open up wide). Then you settle on the one real problem, such as “we need food everyone can eat” (you narrow down). Next you dream up lots of options like pizza, tacos or a picnic (open up again), and finally you try a couple of them out on a small scale and keep the best (narrow down again). That open, close, open, close shape, drawn as two diamonds, is the Double Diamond: Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver. Opening wide is divergent thinking and narrowing down is convergent thinking. It is not a straight line: you can loop back and repeat phases, because “no idea is ever finished”.

Example: Designing a bus-ticket app. Discover: chat with riders and learn they miss buses while digging for coins. Define: “riders need to pay in a few seconds”. Develop: sketch lots of ideas (tap a card, scan a QR code, pay by phone), borrowing from other apps and brainstorming. Deliver: test two of them on one bus route, drop the weak one and improve the winner.

Remember: First diamond = find the right problem (Discover, Define). Second diamond = find the right solution (Develop, Deliver). Each diamond = open wide, then focus. The four design principles: focus on People, Communication, Collaboration, Iteration (think of a good team: it keeps people in mind, keeps talking, works together and keeps improving). The Double Diamond is also supported by a methods bank plus engagement and leadership.

Four phases in two diamonds. Each diamond opens with divergent thinking (considering the problem widely) and closes with convergent thinking (focusing on a particular response).

PhaseWhat happens
1. DiscoverUnderstand the problem rather than assume, by talking to the people affected by it
2. DefineUse the insight from Discover to define the design challenge (often in a different way)
3. DevelopCreate different responses, inspired by competing products, insights, brainstorming
4. DeliverTest different solutions at small scale; reject or improve them
  • Not linear: phases are iterated. “In an ever-changing digital world, no idea is ever finished.”
  • Supported by design principles, a methods bank, and two traits of organization culture: engagement and leadership.
  • Four design principles: focus on people, communication, collaboration, iteration.

Who to involve

In plain words

A new school playground affects lots of people: the kids who play there, parents, teachers, the head teacher who approves the money, even the neighbours who hear the noise. Everyone who can affect a project, or who will be affected by whether it succeeds or fails, is a stakeholder. A product owner is the person who sorts through what users and customers ask for and decides which features get built first, but one person has a limited view and can't predict how real users will react, so real users should be involved at every stage. Involving users gives two wins: expectation management (they see the product early, so there are no nasty surprises at launch) and ownership (people who helped build it feel it is partly theirs, so they support it). How much to involve users is still a puzzle: a review by Abelein et al. (2013) found it helps, but the results vary too much to measure clearly, so the key is knowing who, when, where and how.

Example: Involvement can be big or small: one person face to face or hundreds online, part-time or full-time, or just personas (made-up typical users) when real people aren't available. In the lecture's example, a team designed vibration warnings for a car driver's seat together with users. That is participatory design (co-design). The Windows error-reporting box is another way to collect feedback from huge numbers of users online.

Remember: Expectation management = “no surprises” (about what users expect). Ownership = “I helped make this, so I'll back it” (about what users feel is theirs). To find stakeholders, ask three questions: who is interested? Who has influence or control? Who will be affected?

  • Stakeholders: individuals or groups who can influence or be influenced by the success or failure of a project/product (customers, managers, policymakers, supervisors). Ask: who is interested, who has influence/control, who will be affected? Make a list to decide who to involve and how much.
  • Product owner: filters user/customer input into the development cycle and prioritizes requirements (common in commercial projects). (−) limited view; can't predict how customers respond. So involve potential users across all stages.

Expectation management

Making sure expectations of the new product are realistic, so there are no negative surprises at release. Users see the product's capabilities early.

Ownership

Users who are involved and feel they contributed get a sense of ownership, so they are more likely to support its use.
  • Dilemma: how much involvement, in what role? Abelein et al. (2013) review: involvement has a positive effect, but large variation means no consistent way to measure it; studies reporting negative correlations are 10+ years old. Key: who, when, where, how.
  • Degrees of involvement: face-to-face individual vs hundreds online; part-time vs full-time; personas when involvement is limited. Online: feedback exchange systems, crowdsourcing, citizen engagement, participatory design (co-design). Consider individual circumstances and customer experience (interacting with a brand, not just a product).
  • Examples: Windows error-reporting system; participatory design of vibration warnings on a car seat.

Gould & Lewis (1985): three principles

In plain words

Think of a chef opening a new restaurant. A good chef first learns who the diners are (spice lovers? kids? vegetarians?). Then they let people taste the food and actually watch and count what gets eaten instead of guessing. Then they tweak the recipe and taste again until it's right. In 1985, John Gould and Clayton Lewis wrote down these same three rules for building useful, easy-to-use computer systems: early focus on users and tasks, empirical measurement and iterative design. These three rules are the foundation of the user-centered approach, where people's tasks, goals and needs (not the technology) steer every decision, and people are consulted from the very start to the very end.

Example: Making a banking app. (1) Early focus: study the real users first: how they think (cognitive), what they do (behavioral), their bodies, such as hand size and reach (the slide calls this anthropomorphic), and how they feel about apps (attitudinal). (2) Empirical measurement: first show them printed scenarios or a paper manual and watch how they react, then give them a clickable prototype, time how long a money transfer takes and count the mistakes. (3) Iterative design: fix the confusing button, then test again to check that the fix really helped.

Remember: Know the users → Measure them → Repeat. “Empirical” just means “based on what you actually observe and measure, not on guesses”. Exam trap: “product-owner prioritization” is not one of the three.

  1. Early focus on users and tasks: directly study users' cognitive, behavioral, anthropomorphic and attitudinal characteristics.
  2. Empirical measurement: observe and measure reactions/performance to scenarios and manuals, then to simulations and prototypes.
  3. Iterative design: test and observe again to see the effect of fixes.

These are the basis of a user-centered approach, extended to: tasks and goals drive development; behavior and context are studied; people's characteristics are designed for; people are consulted from start to finish; all decisions are made in context; usability/UX goals are identified, documented and agreed at the start; iteration through data gathering, design and evaluation.

Four basic activities

In plain words

Baking a birthday cake for a friend goes like this: find out what they like and can eat (chocolate? no nuts?), think up a few different cake ideas, make a mini test version to show people, then taste it, get opinions and go back to fix things. Interaction design has the same four basic activities: discovering requirements, designing alternatives that meet them, prototyping those alternatives so they can be shown and judged, and evaluating the product and its user experience all the way through. They form a loop, not a straight line. A lifecycle model is a diagram that shows how the activities connect and repeat.

Example: Making a study-timer app: list what students need (requirements) → sketch three different timer screens (alternatives) → build quick clickable mock-ups (prototypes) → let a few students try them and watch where they get stuck (evaluating) → loop back and improve. The Design Sprint and Research in the Wild are two other lifecycle models that organise this work in their own way.

Remember: Need it → Design it → Mock it up → Test it, then loop. Don't mix these up with the Double Diamond (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver). The four activities are requirements, alternatives, prototyping, evaluating.

  1. Discovering requirements
  2. Designing alternatives that meet them
  3. Prototyping the alternatives so they can be communicated and assessed
  4. Evaluating the product and its UX throughout

A simple lifecycle model links these in an iterative loop (Discover → Design ⇄ Prototype → Evaluate → final product). Other models: Design Sprint, Research in the Wild.

Practical issues

In plain words

Real design projects run into five everyday questions. (1) What do people need? Sometimes the answer is simply to put something out there and see how people react. (2) What should we design? First understand the problem space: what the current experience is like, and why and how it should change. (3) How do we get different ideas? Use creativity, for example the SCAMPER checklist. (4) How do we pick the best idea? Discuss it, build prototypes, run an A/B test, and judge the quality. (5) How do we fit design work into the other development processes (lifecycle models) a company already uses?

Example: An A/B test: a shop's website shows half its visitors a green “Buy” button (version A) and the other half a red one (version B). After a week, the team compares how many people bought something in each group and keeps the winning version.

Remember: Make ideas with SCAMPER. Pick between ideas with A/B testing. Exam trap: A/B testing does not generate alternatives; it chooses among them.

  1. How to find out what people need
  2. How to decide what to design: understand the problem space (current UX, why and how to change it)
  3. How to generate alternatives: creativity (e.g. SCAMPER)
  4. How to choose among alternatives: discussion, prototyping, A/B testing (two versions shown to different users, compare results), quality
  5. How to integrate IxD with other lifecycle models
Lecture 3

Conceptualizing Interaction

Conceptualize what the product will do (a proof of concept). Show where ideas came from, what inspired them, and any theory supporting them. Asking questions, reconsidering assumptions and articulating concerns are central to early ideation.

Assumptions and claims

In plain words

Before you build something new, your head is full of ideas you haven't checked yet. Something you take for granted without proof is an assumption. A prediction of what will happen if you build or change something is a claim. Designers are told to write both down and then defend them with evidence, so the team doesn't build on a guess.

Example: A software engineer thinks: “Everyone goes back to websites through their history, like I do.” That is his assumption. So he says: “We can drop the bookmark feature, nobody needs it.” That is his claim, and it rests on a guess about himself.

Remember: Assumption = what you believe now (taken for granted). Claim = what you predict will happen (“if we do X, then Y”). Then ask the 4 core questions: Is there a problem? Why do you think so? What is your evidence? How will your design fix it?

Assumption

Taking something for granted that requires further investigation.

Claim

Stating what would happen in the future if an action were taken or a product introduced.

Example: an engineer claims bookmarks are no longer needed because he assumes most people revisit sites through their history like he does.

Core questions: Is there a problem with an existing product/UX? Why do you think so? What evidence? How would your design overcome it?

Conceptual models

In plain words

Think of how you'd explain your phone's photo app to a friend in a few seconds: “There are photos and albums. An album holds lots of photos. Delete a photo and it waits in a Recently Deleted folder.” That quick big-picture explanation is a conceptual model: a high-level description of how a system is organized and how it works (Johnson & Henderson, 2002). It has four parts: a metaphor (an album, like a real photo album), concepts (the things, their details, and what you can do to them), relationships (an album holds photos) and mappings (how all of that links to what you actually experience).

Example: The computer desktop: metaphor = a real office desk; concepts = file, folder, trash; relationship = a folder contains files (folders can sit inside folders); mapping = it feels like handling real paper documents in an office. A spreadsheet: concepts = cell, formula, reference; relationship = one cell refers to another; mapping = change one number and every calculation that uses it updates by itself.

Remember: Four parts: Metaphor, Concepts, Relationships, Mappings (“My Cat Reads Maps”). The best model is obvious and simple and works as a shared blueprint (text or diagram); brand-new models are rare. Don't confuse it with a mental model (the picture inside the user's head, Lecture 4).

  • Model: a simplified description of a system or process that helps describe how it works.
  • Conceptual model: a high-level description of how a system is organized and operates (Johnson & Henderson, 2002). Outlines what people can do with a product and what concepts they need to understand to interact with it.

Core components:

  1. Metaphors and analogies: convey what the product is for and how to use it (browsing, bookmarking)
  2. Concepts: task-domain objects, their attributes, and the operations on them (saving, revisiting, organizing)
  3. Relationships between concepts (a folder contains files)
  4. Mappings between concepts and the UX the product supports (revisit a page via history, most-visited, or saved sites)

Interface metaphors

In plain words

A metaphor explains something new by borrowing something you already know. A website's shopping cart is not a real cart, but you instantly get it: put things in, pay later. That is an interface metaphor: a familiar thing that helps people quickly understand the conceptual model underneath, and it is a central part of that model. The catch: the digital version may not behave like the real one, so a metaphor can contravene (go against) people's expectations, and for many tasks, like buying a ticket or finding a reference, a good visual metaphor is hard to find (Alan Cooper).

Example: Metaphors are used in three ways: to describe an activity (live streaming, like a flowing stream), as the model of the whole interface (apps built from swipeable cards), and to picture one action (a cart icon you drop items into).

Remember: Three uses = name it, build it, show it: name the activity (streaming), build the screen around it (cards), show one action as a picture (cart icon). Main downside: it can contravene expectations.

  • A central component of a conceptual model. Provide familiar entities so people can readily understand the underlying model (search engine, shopping cart).
  • Downsides: they can contravene people's expectations; useful visual metaphors for many tasks (buying a ticket, finding a reference) are hard to find (Alan Cooper).
  • Used in three ways: (1) to conceptualize what we are doing (live streaming); (2) as a conceptual model at the interface level (the card metaphor); (3) to visualize an operation (shopping-cart icon).

Five interaction types

In plain words

There are five basic ways a person and a system can deal with each other. You can give it orders (instructing), chat with it (conversing), grab and move things as if they were real (manipulating), move around inside a space (exploring), or the system speaks up first and you react (responding). Deciding which types to use, and being able to say why, helps a designer think through the options.

Example: On your phone: tapping Delete on an email (instructing), asking Siri “Will it rain today?” (conversing), pinching a photo to zoom in (manipulating), walking down a street in a 3D map view (exploring), and your watch buzzing “Time to stand up” (responding).

Remember: Order: Instruct, Converse, Manipulate, Explore, Respond (“I Can Make Everything Respond”). Only Responding starts with the system: the system takes the initiative.

Why it's in the courseA worked example of an interface metaphor (Lecture 3): it borrows the drawing-software (Photoshop) model of tools, layers and direct manipulation and applies it to text. Its user study is also a within-subject comparison (each of the 12 participants rated both conditions).

Problems with current text editors

In plain words

Most writing apps copy one old idea: a sheet of paper. Paper is fine for reading, but clumsy when you want to play with text. The paper names four problems: organizing is limited when you rely only on the paper metaphor, exploring different versions of a sentence is hard, moving ideas you already wrote is tedious, and keeping track of different versions of a text is tedious.

Example: You wrote a long email and want to try a friendlier tone and a shorter length. In a normal editor you copy, paste and rewrite by hand, keep several drafts, and soon lose track of which draft is which.

Remember: 4 problems: Paper, Variations, Reorganizing, Versions. Textoshop's fix: swap the paper metaphor for a drawing-software metaphor (Photoshop).

  1. Organizing is limited when relying only on the sheet-of-paper metaphor
  2. Exploring possible text variations is difficult
  3. Reorganizing ideas already written is tedious
  4. Managing and rendering different versions is tedious

Related work: non-linear editors (hypertext, Scrivener); LLM variations; reordering (Langsmith, VISAR); versioning (ABScribe, Google Docs).

Taxonomy of writing changes

In plain words

A taxonomy is just a sorted list of kinds, like sorting laundry into piles. The authors sorted the edits writers often make into kinds: formal changes (spelling, tense, number), meaning-preserving changes (say the same thing another way: swap in a synonym, reorder words), and micro vs macro changes (edits at different scales, from one word up to the whole text). This list of frequent writing tasks shows which edits a better editor should make easy.

Example: “The cat sits” → “The cats sat” is a formal change (number and tense). “The movie was great” → “The film was excellent” is meaning-preserving (synonyms). Fixing one word is micro; restructuring a whole story is macro.

Remember: Formal = the form changes (spelling, tense, number). Meaning-preserving = same meaning, new words or order. Micro vs macro = small scale vs big scale.

Formal changes (spelling, tense, number) · meaning-preserving changes (synonyms, reordering) · micro vs macro changes (different scales of writing).

Features

In plain words

Textoshop treats text like a picture in Photoshop. You can grab a passage and drag it, resize it (shrink the box and the text gets shorter, stretch it and it gets longer), rotate it (to rephrase or reorder) or split it. You also get paint-style tools (Tone Brush, Tone Eyedropper, Eraser and more), a Tone Picker that works like a color wheel, Boolean operations that combine passages the way shape tools combine shapes, and layers to stack and compare versions. Grabbing and changing things directly like this is called direct manipulation.

Example: Like painting a wall: the Tone Brush “paints” a new tone onto a casual sentence, and the Tone Eyedropper “picks up” the tone of a sentence you like so you can use it elsewhere, just as an eyedropper picks a color in Photoshop.

Remember: Boolean ops as two overlapping circles: Unite = both circles; Intersect = only the overlap; Subtract = one circle minus the other; Exclude = everything except the overlap. Insert just drops a passage in a new spot.

  • Direct manipulation: drag, resize (shorten/expand text), rotate (rephrase/reorder), split.
  • Tools: Tone Brush (changes tone) · Tone Eyedropper (copies tone from one passage) · Smudge (regenerates/smooths) · Singularizer/Pluralizer (number + agreement) · Past/Present/Future Tense Changer · Repair (grammar, spelling, punctuation) · Eraser (deletes but keeps the sentence correct) · Prompt (LLM prompt inserts text).
  • Tone Picker: a color wheel of tones + sliders informal↔formal, negative↔positive, complicated↔simple.
  • Boolean operations on sentences: Unite (combine both) · Intersect (keep only shared ideas) · Subtract (remove one passage's ideas from another) · Exclude (drop overlap, keep non-redundant ideas) · Insert (drop passage in a new position).
  • Text layers: stack edits as layers and toggle visibility to compare versions.

User study

In plain words

To check whether Textoshop really helps, the authors ran a user study. 12 people used both a normal comparison version (the Baseline) and Textoshop, so every person tried both; that is a within-subject design. The study included a video tutorial and 4 writing tasks. People filled in the SUS (System Usability Scale, a standard usability questionnaire) and 5-point questions about how difficult things felt, then explored freely and had a semi-structured interview (planned questions plus free follow-up questions). Textoshop was rated easier on every item.

Example: It works like a taste test where every friend tries both pizzas and rates each one. Here each participant took part remotely (a website + Zoom) and received a CAD$20 gift card.

Remember: 12 · 2 · 4: 12 participants, 2 conditions (Baseline vs Textoshop), 4 tasks (shorten/expand a story; organize and integrate text fragments; change the tone of dialogue; write emails from a template). Measures: SUS + 5-point difficulty + interview.

  • 12 participants, remote (website + Zoom), CAD$20 gift card.
  • Conditions: Baseline vs Textoshop. 4 tasks: shorten/expand a story; organize and integrate fragments; change the tone of dialogue; write emails from a template.
  • Measures: SUS (System Usability Scale) + 5-point difficulty questions; free-form exploration + semi-structured interview.
  • Result: Textoshop rated easier on every item (e.g. precisely shortening/expanding text, lower effort, lower mental demand, less frustration).
Lecture 4

Cognitive Aspects

What is cognition?

In plain words

Cognition is a big word for everything your mind does with information: noticing, seeing, remembering, learning, reading, talking, deciding, even daydreaming. Norman (1993) split it into two modes. Experiential cognition is autopilot: you see, act and react smoothly, without effort. Reflective cognition is “stop and think hard”: it takes effort, attention, judgment and decisions, and it is where new ideas and creativity come from. The lecture also lists the main processes (attention, perception, memory, learning, reading/speaking/listening, problem-solving/planning/reasoning/decision-making) and says they are interdependent: they almost always work together, rarely one alone.

Example: Riding your usual bus home while chatting is experiential: you barely think about it. Planning a brand-new trip across the city with two transfers is reflective: you stop, compare options and decide. Even just reading the bus sign uses perception, attention and memory at the same time.

Remember: Experiential = autopilot (easy, fast). Reflective = reflect = stop and think (effortful, creative). Both come from Norman, 1993.

  • Thinking, remembering, learning, daydreaming, decision-making, seeing, reading, writing, talking…
  • Two modes (Norman, 1993): Experiential: perceive, act and react intuitively and effortlessly. Reflective: mental effort, attention, judgment, decision-making; leads to new ideas and creativity.
  • Processes (Eysenck & Brysbaert, 2018): attention · perception · memory · learning · reading/speaking/listening · problem-solving/planning/reasoning/decision-making. They are interdependent; rarely one alone.

Attention

In plain words

Your eyes and ears are hit by lots of things at once, but you can only focus on a few. Attention is choosing what to concentrate on right now, out of everything available, so you can focus on what matters for your task. It is easier when (1) you have a clear goal and (2) the thing you need is salient, meaning it stands out. Can you do several things at once without one of them suffering? It depends on the tasks and how much attention each one needs, and heavy media multitaskers get distracted more easily. So designers should make important information stand out when it is needed (color, animation, underlining, ordering, spacing), avoid cluttered screens, and help people switch away and come back.

Example: In a busy food court, if you already know you want pizza (clear goal) and the pizza shop has a big bright sign (salient), you find it in seconds. If you are “just looking for something” and every sign looks the same, it takes much longer.

Remember: Attention is easy with Goal + Glow: a clear goal, and information that stands out. Studies: Bowman 2010 = messaging students took up to 50% longer to read; Uncapher & Wagner 2018 = no relationship; Lottridge 2015 = more complex (distractions can help if they relate to the task).

Selecting things to concentrate on, at a point in time, from the available range. Ease depends on: (1) whether you have clear goals and (2) whether the needed info is salient in the environment.

  • Multitasking: whether performance suffers depends on the nature of the tasks and how much attention each demands. Heavy media multitaskers are more easily distracted; it overloads the capacity to focus.
  • Bowman et al. 2010: students messaging took up to 50% longer to read a passage. Uncapher & Wagner 2018: no relationship. Lottridge et al. 2015: more complex; heavy multitaskers can benefit if distractions are task-relevant.
  • Design: make important info noticeable when needed (animation, color, underlining, ordering, spacing); don't overcomplicate; support switching and returning (gradually brightening lights, alert sounds).

Perception

In plain words

Perception is how the raw signals from your five senses (sight, hearing, touch, taste, smell) are turned into experiences you understand, like “that is a cup” or “that is my phone ringing”. The lecture adds kinesthesia: knowing where your body parts are and how they move, thanks to sensors in your muscles and joints called proprioceptors. Perception works together with memory, attention and language, and for most people vision is the most dominant sense, then hearing, then touch. So designers must show information in a way that is easy to see, hear or feel, and easy to tell apart.

Example: You can touch your nose with your eyes closed: that is kinesthesia. A well-designed phone uses icons that look clearly different, white space to group things, different sounds for a message and a call, and strong color contrast so people with color-vision problems can still read the text.

Remember: Sense ranking: See > Hear > Touch. Kinesthesia = “where is my body?” (position + movement).

How information is acquired from the environment through the five senses and transformed into experiences (Roth, 1986). Plus kinesthesia: awareness of body position and movement via proprioceptors. Involves other processes (memory, attention, language). Dominance: vision > hearing > touch.

Design: icons easy to tell apart; white space to group info; distinct audio cues; good color contrast (consider color-vision deficiency); haptics used sparingly and distinctly.

Memory

In plain words

Memory is pulling up the knowledge you need so you can act properly. Your brain does not keep everything: a filtering process (driven by what you pay attention to) decides what gets stored, and it makes mistakes. Working memory holds a small amount of recent information for a short time, about 7 ± 2 chunks; long-term memory holds things from long ago. It is much easier to recognize something you can see than to recall it from nothing, so good apps show you choices instead of making you remember them. The lecture also flags passwords (easy to remember can mean easy to guess), digital forgetting (helping people forget, like removing an ex-partner's photos after a breakup) and SenseCam, a wearable camera that takes photos through the day to help people with memory loss look back.

Example: Working memory is like a small desk: only about 7 things fit before stuff falls off. That is why phone numbers are split into groups (010-1234-5678 is 3 chunks, not 11 digits). And bumping into your teacher at the supermarket and thinking “I know that face... from where?” shows the encoding context effect: you stored that face in a different place, the classroom.

Remember: Memory factors = F-C-R-M: Filtering (attention), encoding Context, Recognition beats recall, Modality (we remember visual cues well). Design: short, simple tasks; recognition over recall; labels, folders, tags, colors, icons.

  • Recalling knowledge that lets us act appropriately. A filtering process decides what is remembered (not perfect).
  • Working memory (recent, small amount) vs long-term memory. Magic number 7 ± 2 chunks.
  • Factors: attention/filtering (doing exercises, discussing, notes help); encoding context (a familiar person in an unfamiliar place is hard to place); recognition is easier than recall; modality (visual cues are remembered well). Photo-taking impairment effect.
  • Considerations: passwords (memory load vs vulnerability); digital forgetting (helping people forget, e.g. after a breakup); memory loss support (SenseCam).
  • Design: keep tasks short and simple; support recognition (familiar menus, icons, consistent layouts); labels, folders, tags, colors to find info.

Learning

In plain words

Learning is building up skills and knowledge, and it needs memory: if nothing stuck, you could never learn anything. Some learning just happens without trying; this is incidental learning, like learning a face or the streets near your home. Some learning is on purpose, with a goal; this is intentional learning, like studying a foreign language or learning to drive. You can learn by being told (instruction) or by doing, and interactive technology gives people new ways to learn by doing.

Example: Studying for this midterm is intentional learning; knowing where the café is on campus without ever trying to learn it is incidental. A science-museum exhibit you can touch and play with (the lecture shows a 2024 study comparing physical and haptic exhibits) lets you learn by doing instead of only reading a sign.

Remember: Incidental = “I didn't mean to.” Intentional = with an intention, a goal.

Incidental (no intention: faces, streets) vs intentional (goal-directed: languages, driving). Learning by instruction vs by doing; interactive tech offers alternative ways to learn.

Cognitive frameworks

In plain words

A cognitive framework is a way of explaining how people think when they use technology. This section has several, each with one core idea:
Mental model (Craik, 1943): the “how it works” picture in your head that you use to predict what will happen.
Gulfs (Norman, 1986; Hutchins et al., 1986): the gap from you to the system (execution: “how do I do it?”) and back from the system to you (evaluation: “what happened?”).
Information processing model: the 1980s idea that the mind works like a computer, in ordered stages; not used nowadays.
Cognitive load: how much mental effort learning something new takes, measured with NASA-TLX.
Distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995): people + tools + environment studied as one thinking system.
External cognition (Scaife & Rogers, 1996): using things outside your head (lists, reminders, paper) to think with less effort. Extended mind (Clark & Chalmers, 1998): digital tech becomes part of your mind.
Embodied interaction (Dourish, 2001): we think and make meaning through our bodies and hands-on contact with the world.

Example: Coming home to a cold house, you turn the thermostat to max because “more is more”. But the heater warms at the same speed whatever number you set, so your mental model is wrong. Writing a shopping list instead of memorizing it is external cognition (cognitive offloading).

Remember: Who said what: Craik = mental model · Norman = gulfs · Hutchins = distributed · Scaife & Rogers = external · Clark & Chalmers = extended mind · Dourish = embodied. NASA-TLX six: Many People Try Pizza Every Friday = Mental, Physical, Temporal, Performance, Effort, Frustration.

  • Mental model (Craik, 1943): internal constructions of the external world that can be manipulated to make predictions and inferences. Develops as you learn more. Often incomplete/erroneous: e.g. cranking the thermostat to max to heat faster (“more is more”), pressing the elevator button repeatedly. People resist spending time learning how things work.
  • Gulfs (Norman 1986; Hutchins et al. 1986): Gulf of execution = distance from the user to the physical system (how do I do it?). Gulf of evaluation = distance from the system back to the user (what happened?). Designers bridge the gulfs to reduce cognitive effort.
  • Information processing model: 1980s metaphor of the mind as an information processor with ordered stages. Not used nowadays.
  • Cognitive load: mental effort required to learn new info; keep it minimal. Measured with NASA-TLX (Hart & Staveland, 1988): six Likert items: mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort, frustration.
  • Distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995): analyzes people, tools and environment as one cognitive system. Example: airplane cockpit (pilot, captain, air-traffic control, instruments, sky/runway). Information is re-represented across media (speech, notes, instruments) = representational state changes. Design: maintain shared awareness and redundancy.
  • External cognition (Scaife & Rogers, 1996): designing tech that empowers cognition while reducing effort. Extended mind (Clark & Chalmers, 1998): philosophical view that digital tech becomes an extension of our minds.

Lecture 5

Data Gathering

Data (quantitative or qualitative) helps understand a design, stakeholders' goals and behavior. Central to discovering requirements and evaluation. Three techniques: interviews, questionnaires, observation.

Six key issues

In plain words

Before you collect any information from people, you plan it, a bit like planning a school trip. The lecture lists six things to sort out first, the six key issues:
1. Goal setting: decide what you want to find out. The goal decides which method you use.
2. Identifying participants (sampling): choose who to ask. Probability sampling picks people by chance: random (names from a hat) or stratified (split people into groups first, then pick at random inside each group). Nonprobability sampling, such as convenience sampling, just uses whoever is around. The big difference: only chance-picked samples let you generalize (say “this is probably true for everyone”).
3. Relationship with participants: be clear and professional. Get informed consent (they agree after being told what will happen), build rapport (a relaxed, trusting feel) and offer incentives (a small reward for taking part).
4. Ethics: store the data safely (is that recording on your laptop encrypted?). Telling people too much about what you hope to find causes bias; hiding things or giving false information is an ethical problem.
5. Triangulation: check the same thing from different angles so you can trust it (reliability). Vary the data (sources, times, places, people), the investigators (researchers), the theories (frameworks) or the methods (techniques).
6. Pilot study: a small practice run first, to find and fix problems. People who took part in the pilot can't join the main study.

Example: Testing an alarm app. Goal: do people wake up on time, and is setting an alarm easy? Participants: a random sample of students, not just your roommates. Relationship: explain the study, get a signed consent form, offer a coffee voucher. Ethics: keep recordings on an encrypted drive. Triangulation: compare the app's logs with a questionnaire and a few interviews. Pilot: try everything on two friends first, who then sit out the real study.

Remember: The six in order: Goal, Participants, Relationship, Ethics, Triangulation, Pilot: “Good Plans Really Ease The Project.” Sampling in one line: by chance (random, stratified) = can generalize; whoever is handy (convenience) = can't. Too much info → bias; hidden or false info → ethics problem.

  1. Goal setting: the goal decides the technique.
  2. Identifying participants (sampling)
    • Probability: random (chosen randomly); stratified (split population into groups, e.g. school classes, then randomly sample each).
    • Nonprobability: convenience (whoever is available).
    • Difference: whether results can be generalized.
  3. Relationship with participants: clear, professional; informed consent (privacy, trust); rapport (relaxed, trusting); incentives.
  4. Ethical considerations: store data securely (encrypted?); too much info about the study → bias; hidden or false info → ethical problems.
  5. Triangulation (for reliability): data (different sources, times, places, people) · investigator (different researchers collect and interpret) · theories (different theoretical frameworks) · methodological (different data-gathering techniques).
  6. Pilot study: small trial run to find and fix problems (and predict results). Pilot participants cannot take part in the main study.

Capturing data

In plain words

Once people start talking or doing things, you need a way to keep a record, like taking notes in class or recording a voice memo. Some methods record themselves (a questionnaire is already written down; an interaction log saves clicks automatically), so nothing extra is needed. Otherwise you choose based on your goal, the setting, and how much time and money you have.
Notes + photos: the simplest and most flexible, though a bit old-fashioned.
Audio + photos: a voice recorder feels less intrusive (less in-your-face) than a camera, for both sides, but the recording can be hard to make sense of later (background noise, mumbled or confusing words).
Video: the best quality, but long recordings need huge storage, it is hard to go through (“decode”) because so much is happening in each scene, and it can raise privacy and ethical problems. Sometimes it is required anyway, for example in studies with disabled participants or field studies.

Example: Interviewing a café owner about a new ordering app. Jotting notes and snapping a photo of the counter is quick and easy. A voice recorder lets them relax more than a camera would, but the coffee grinder might drown out some words. A video catches every gesture, but fills your phone and shows their face, so privacy becomes an issue.

Remember: Audio vs video is a trade-off. Audio = less intrusive, harder to interpret. Video = best quality, but huge storage, hard to decode, privacy issues.

  • Some methods need no capture (questionnaires, interaction logs). Choice depends on goal, context, resources, time.
  • Notes + photos: least technical, most flexible.
  • Audio + photos: less intrusive than video, but harder to interpret afterwards (noise, unclear words).
  • Video: good quality, needs huge storage, sometimes mandatory (disabled participants, field studies), hard to decode, privacy issues.

Interviews: “a conversation with a purpose” (Kahn & Cannell, 1957)

In plain words

An interview is a chat with a goal: “a conversation with a purpose”. The four kinds differ in how tightly you stick to a script.
Unstructured (open-ended): like a free chat on a topic, with open questions and probing follow-ups (“Can you tell me more?”). You get rich, surprising answers, but every chat goes differently and they take ages to analyze.
Structured: like reading a form out loud. The same short, mostly closed questions in the same order for everyone. Quick, and easy to compare.
Semi-structured: the middle way. A basic script so everyone covers the same topics, mixing closed and open questions. Avoid leading questions, watch your body language and tone, and use neutral follow-ups.
Focus group: 3–10 people interviewed together, led by a trained facilitator (host). You hear many views and spot shared problems and disagreements, but the group may drift into the same opinion (groupthink) or a few loud people may take over.
Good questions are short, ask one thing at a time, avoid jargon and stay neutral. An interview runs in five stages: Introduction → Warm-up → Main session → Cooling-off → Closing.

Example: Asking people about streaming. Structured: “How often do you watch streamed content: every day, weekly, monthly, or less often?” Unstructured: “Tell me about the last time you watched something online,” then probe with “Can you tell me more about that?” Leading (avoid): “Don't you love the new playlist feature?” Neutral: “What do you think of the playlist feature?”

Remember: How much script? Structured = full script, semi-structured = half a script, unstructured = no script, focus group = a group with a host. The five stages are like having a guest over: hello and explain (Introduction) → small talk (Warm-up) → the real conversation (Main) → “anything else?” (Cooling-off) → thanks and goodbye (Closing).

Questionnaires

In plain words

A questionnaire is a written list of questions people answer on their own, like the feedback form at the end of a course. It collects lots of answers cheaply and works best when people are motivated to fill it in. A structured interview (similar questions, but read out by a person) is better when you need to persuade busy people, choose who you ask, or explain a confusing question, but it costs much more researcher time.
Order: put demographics (age, experience) first, then the main questions grouped by topic. The order of questions can change the answers. Keep it short and relevant, give clear instructions, use white space, let people opt out of long ones, and consider different versions for different groups of people.
Closed questions give ready-made answers (add “No opinion” or “None of these”). Online, radio buttons allow one choice, check boxes allow many, and drop-down menus hide the list until you open it. Ranges like age groups must not overlap (15–19, 20–24), but they don't need to be the same size.
Rating scales measure attitudes and opinions. A Likert scale asks how much you agree with a statement (1 strongly disagree … 5 strongly agree). A semantic differential puts two opposite words at the ends (Useful … Useless) and you mark a point in between; swap which side the positive word is on now and then, to reduce bias.

Example: A food-delivery app survey: first “How old are you? 17 or younger / 18–25 / 26 and older” (unequal but non-overlapping ranges). Then “Which device do you use most often?” with radio buttons (one answer), “Which cuisines do you order? Tick all that apply” with check boxes, the Likert item “The app is easy to use” (1–5), and a semantic differential “Fast ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ Slow”.

Remember: Radio buttons = one choice, like an old car radio where pressing one station button pops the others out. Check boxes = many, like ticking a to-do list. Likert asks “Do you agree?”; semantic differential asks “Which of two opposite words is it closer to?”

  • Large-scale data efficiently; closed + open questions; best when respondents are motivated to answer on their own.
  • vs structured interview: interview is better when persuasion is needed and lets you pick participants and clarify ambiguity, but costs more researcher time.
  • Structure: demographics first, then goal-related questions grouped by topic. Question order can influence answers. Different versions for different populations; clear instructions; concise; allow opt-outs; use white space.
  • Closed questions: include “No opinion / None of these”. Radio buttons = one choice; check boxes = multiple; drop-downs = hidden lists.
  • Ranges: don't overlap (15–19, 20–24, not 15–20, 20–25). Intervals needn't be equal.
  • Likert scale: agreement with a statement (1 strongly disagree … 5 strongly agree). Steps: gather a pool of short statements → decide the scale (points, layout) → select and reword items.
  • Semantic differential: bipolar adjective pairs (Useful–Useless, Clear–Confusing); mix positive/negative sides to reduce bias.

Levels of measurement

In plain words

Numbers in a survey don't all behave the same way, so the kind of number decides which maths and which test you may use. There are four levels of measurement, and each one adds something:
Nominal = just names or labels, no order (device type: phone, laptop, tablet). You can only count them and find the most common one (the mode).
Ordinal = has an order, but the gaps between steps aren't guaranteed to be equal (a 1–5 satisfaction rating). Use the middle value (the median) and ranks.
Interval = order and equal gaps, but no true zero (temperature in °C: 0 °C doesn't mean “no temperature”). You can add, subtract and take the mean (average).
Ratio = equal gaps and a true zero (task time: 0 seconds really means no time). All maths works, so “twice as long” makes sense.
Matching tests: nominal → chi-square; ordinal → Mann-Whitney U (2 groups) or Kruskal-Wallis (3+ groups); interval → t-test or ANOVA (if their assumptions are met); ratio → t-test, ANOVA, plus correlation and regression.
Unipolar vs bipolar: a rating scale is unipolar when it goes from nothing up to a lot of one thing (Not at all satisfied … Very satisfied), and bipolar when it runs from a negative side, through neutral, to a positive side (Very dissatisfied … Very satisfied). Don't mix both types in one survey.

Example: One survey, four levels: “Which phone do you use?” (nominal), “Rate the app 1–5” (ordinal), a combined questionnaire score such as SUS or NASA-TLX (treated as interval), and the app's log of how many seconds checkout took (ratio). Wording matters too: “How satisfied are you?” names only the positive side (it fits a unipolar scale), while “How satisfied or dissatisfied are you?” names both sides (it fits a bipolar scale).

Remember: NOIR (French for “black”): Nominal → Ordinal → Interval → Ratio adds names → order → equal gaps → true zero. Zero test: “Does 0 mean none at all?” Yes → ratio (0 seconds). No → interval (0 °C). Rules of thumb: who people are (categories) → nominal; survey ratings → ordinal; SUS / NASA-TLX → interval; system logs (time, clicks) → ratio.

Observation

In plain words

Observation means watching what people actually do, not just what they say they do. It can be direct (you watch them live) or indirect (you study records afterwards: logs, diaries, video, analytics). It can happen in the wild (their normal everyday setting) or controlled (set tasks in a lab).
Direct, in the wild: you see real behavior in its real context, but it's messy and lots happens at once, so a checklist framework keeps you focused (for example Person, Place, Thing). You can be a passive observer (an outsider just watching) or a participant observer (joining in as an insider: richer insight, but harder to stay objective). Ethnography is the long, deep version: joining a group over a long time, mixing observation, interviews and collected artifacts, and treating everything as “strange” so nothing is taken for granted.
Direct, controlled (lab): follow a script so every session is the same, often film the face, screen and body together, and use think-aloud: people say their thoughts out loud while doing the task, which shows what is going on in their head. Talking can slightly change how they perform, so treat everyone the same way.
Indirect: diaries (people write about their own experiences over days or weeks: little researcher time, but people forget details or stop filling them in), ESM (experience sampling: people get a prompt, often on their phone, at random or chosen moments and answer about that moment right then), and interaction logs (the system quietly records keys, clicks, time and navigation, at huge scale).

Example: Studying a bus app. Direct in the wild: ride the bus and watch people check times. Participant observer: commute with them every day for weeks. Lab + think-aloud: “Find the next bus to the station, and say what you're thinking,” and you hear “Hmm, I expected the map to be here…”. Diary: riders note each trip in the evening. ESM: a ping at a random time asks “Are you waiting for a bus right now? How do you feel?” Logs: the app records every tap.

Remember: Two switches: direct ↔ indirect (watch live vs study records) and in the wild ↔ controlled (real life vs lab). Diary = you write when you remember; ESM = your phone asks you right now. Participant observer = insider (rich but less objective); passive observer = outsider.

  • Direct (watch people) vs indirect (study records: logs, diaries, video, analytics). In the wild (natural setting) vs controlled (specified tasks in a lab).
  • Direct, in the wild: + real behavior and context · − complex, messy data. Keep focus with a framework: Person, Place, Thing or Space, Actors, Activities, Objects, Acts, Events, Time, Goals, Feelings.
  • Degree of participation: passive observer (outsider) ↔ participant observer (insider, richer insight but harder to stay objective).
  • Ethnography: long-form participant observation, viewing everything as “strange”; mixes observation, interviews, artifacts. Digital ethnography: technology tours, hybrid online + physical.
  • Direct, controlled: script for consistency; multi-camera (face, interface, body). Think-aloud: participants verbalize goals, expectations, confusion to reveal cognition; can slightly affect performance, so be consistent.
  • Indirect: diaries (+ low researcher time, long studies · − memory and compliance issues); ESM (experience sampling: random or strategic prompts, immediate capture); interaction logs (keys, clicks, time, navigation; unobtrusive, large-scale); social/web traces (scale and ethics issues).

User Study & One-Way ANOVA

Study design (coffee example: which of A, B, C is preferred?)

In plain words

Before testing anything, you decide who tries what. In a between-subject design (BSD) each person tries only one version, so you compare different people with each other. In a within-subject design (WSD) each person tries every version, so each person is compared with themselves. A mixed design uses both at once: when a study changes two things, everyone may try every version of one thing, while the other thing is split across different groups. Each design has a score “recipe” (a model): BSD = grand mean + treatment effect + error, and WSD adds one extra ingredient, the participant effect Sj.

Example: A coffee taste test with 30 people. BSD: 10 people taste only A, 10 only B, 10 only C. WSD: all 30 taste A, B and C, so if P1 simply loves coffee and scores everything high, you can spot that and take it out of the noise. In BSD that person's love of coffee just hides inside the error.

Remember: Between = each person gets one (needs more people, no order effects). Within = each person gets them all (fewer people, but watch order and tiredness, so counterbalance). WSD recipe = BSD recipe + Sj.

Variables

In plain words

A study is a fair test: you change one thing on purpose, measure what happens, and keep everything else the same. The thing you change is the independent variable (the cause). The thing you measure is the dependent variable (the effect). The things you keep fixed are controlled variables, so nothing else can explain the result.

Example: In the coffee study, type of coffee (A, B, C) is the independent variable, the satisfaction score is the dependent variable, and temperature, cups and time are controlled. If C were served hot and A cold, you couldn't tell whether the coffee or the temperature made the difference.

Remember: The dependent variable depends on what you did. Change it = independent. Measure it = dependent. Freeze it = controlled.

  • Independent variable (cause): what the researcher manipulates. Type of coffee.
  • Dependent variable (effect): what is measured. Satisfaction score.
  • Controlled variable (constant): kept the same so results are valid. Temperature, cups, time.

Why ANOVA?

In plain words

Group averages almost always differ a little just by luck, so we need a test that says whether a difference is too big to blame on luck alone (significant). A t-test only compares two groups, so for three or more groups (k ≥ 3) we use one unified test: ANOVA (Analysis of Variance). It starts from the boring default H₀ (no difference among the group means) and only drops it when the data give strong evidence for HA (at least one mean differs). It assumes the leftover noise is bell-shaped (normal) with the same spread in every group: ε ~ N(0, σ²).

Example: Like a court: the judge assumes “innocent” (H₀: all three coffees are liked equally) until the evidence is strong. Weak evidence gives “not guilty” (fail to reject H₀), which is not the same as “proven innocent”.

Remember: t-test = two groups; ANOVA = three or more. H₀ = “nothing going on”. HA = “at least one differs”, not “all differ”.

Tests whether differences between group means are significant. A t-test handles only two groups; for k ≥ 3 groups we need ANOVA (Analysis of Variance).

  • H0: there is no significant difference among the means μ1, μ2, …, μk. HA: at least one mean differs.
  • Assumption: errors εij ~ N(0, σ²) (normally distributed, equal variance).

Splitting the variability

In plain words

Scores are spread out, and ANOVA splits that spread into two parts. Between-group variability (SSB) is how far each group's average is from the grand average: the signal the coffee could cause. Within-group variability (SSW) is how far each score is from its own group's average: noise, because everyone in that group got the same coffee. Each is a sum of squares (find the distances, square them, add them up), and together they make the total: SST = SSB + SSW. You can't compare the two totals directly, because more scores pile up a bigger total, so next we divide by degrees of freedom.

Example: Picture three clouds of dots on a line, one cloud per coffee. Clouds far apart and thin (big SSB, small SSW) look like a real difference; clouds close together and fat look like it could just be luck.

Remember: Between = how far apart the cloud centers are (signal). Within = how fat each cloud is (noise). Don't mix up between/within-group variability with between/within-subject design: different ideas.

Total variability = Between-group variability + Within-group variability  →  SST = SSB + SSW

Degrees of freedom

In plain words

Degrees of freedom (df) count how many numbers are truly free to change. Once an average is fixed, the last number is forced, so n numbers around their own average give only n − 1 free pieces of information. In ANOVA: between groups has k − 1 (k group means around the grand mean), within groups has N − k (each of the k groups loses one), and total has N − 1. Like the sums of squares, they add up: N − 1 = (k − 1) + (N − k).

Example: Three friends share 15 cookies, 5 each on average. Friend 1 takes 4 and friend 2 takes 6, so friend 3 must get 5: no choice left. In the coffee study, k = 3 and N = 12, so df = 2 (between), 9 (within) and 11 (total).

Remember: Between = k − 1, Within = N − k, Total = N − 1. Small k = number of groups, big N = number of all scores. Check: between + within = total.

df = the number of independent pieces of information contributing to an SS. If 3 values have mean 5 and two are known, the third is fixed, so df = n − 1.

dfT = N − 1  =  dfB = k − 1  +  dfW = N − k   (k = # groups, N = # total observations)

F-value

In plain words

First turn each sum of squares into a fair average spread (a variance), called a mean square: MSB = SSB/(k − 1) and MSW = SSW/(N − k). Then F = MSB / MSW: signal divided by noise. If nothing is going on, F usually comes out small, around 1; a big F means the groups differ much more than noise would explain, which points to rejecting H₀. When H₀ is true, F follows a known curve, the F distribution F(k − 1, N − k), which shows how big F gets by luck alone.

Example: Hearing a friend in a noisy cafeteria depends on their voice compared with the room noise. In the coffee study MSB = 127.08 and MSW = 2.50, so F = 50.83: the “voice” is about 51 times the “room noise”.

Remember: F = B over W (between on top, within on the bottom), and always with MS, never SS. Big F = the groups stand out from the noise.

MSB = SSB / (k − 1)    MSW = SSW / (N − k)    F = MSB / MSW ~ F(k − 1, N − k)
  • F compares between-group variance to within-group variance (effect vs noise).
  • Background (formulas given if needed): a sum of n squared N(0,1) variables follows χ²(n); (n−1)S²/σ² ~ χ²(n−1); a ratio of two independent χ² variables, each divided by its df, follows the F distribution.
  • Large F → between-group variance ↑, within-group variance ↓ → more likely significant → reject H0.

p-value and errors

In plain words

The p-value answers: if nothing were really going on (H₀ true), how likely is an F at least as big as ours? It is written p = P(F > Fobs | H₀). Before looking at the data we set a bar α, the significance level (usually 5% or 1%): if p < α the result is significant and we reject H₀, otherwise we fail to reject it. We can be wrong in two ways: a Type I error (false positive: rejecting a true H₀, chance α) or a Type II error (false negative: missing a real difference, chance β). Power = 1 − β is the chance of catching a real difference, and α is fixed first because a Type I error is considered more serious.

Example: A smoke alarm can be wrong in two ways: beeping when there is no fire is a Type I error, and staying silent during a real fire is a Type II error. Choosing α = 0.05 means accepting at most a 5% chance of a false alarm when there really is no fire.

Remember: Boy who cried wolf: Type I = villagers believe him when there is no wolf (false alarm, α). Type II = they ignore him when the wolf is real (miss, β). Power = 1 − β. And: “if p is low, H₀ must go”.

p = P(F > Fobs | H0)

Shortcut: running a BSD ANOVA

In plain words

In real life a computer does the maths. The lecture's recipe: (1) put your results in a table such as Excel, (2) set the independent and dependent variables and the dfs correctly (effect k − 1, error N − k), (3) run the ANOVA in a tool like jamovi, SPSS or R, (4) report the ANOVA table with F and p, and (5) if the effect is significant, run a post-hoc test to find which groups differ. ANOVA alone only says that at least one group is different, not which one.

Example: The coffee result is reported as F(2, 9) = 50.83, p < .001. The two numbers in brackets are the dfs (between, within), so a reader can tell the study had 3 groups and 12 scores.

Remember: Report it like an address: F(dfbetween, dfwithin) = F value, p = …. Significant? Then post-hoc (Latin for “after this”).

  1. Organize the results (Excel etc.).
  2. Set independent and dependent variables correctly; set df: effect = k − 1, error = N − k.
  3. Run it in a tool (jamovi, SPSS, R, SAS, Minitab…).
  4. Report the ANOVA table (F and p), e.g. F(2, 9) = 50.83, p < .001.
  5. If significant, run post-hoc tests to find which groups differ.
